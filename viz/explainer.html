Shapelet privacy study · SAML-D · 3 seeds

How shapelets leak customers' bank histories

Shapelet models explain a money-laundering alert by pointing at a short pattern in an account's activity. That pattern is often a copy of a real customer's transactions. This page walks through the data, the experiment and one real leak.

1 · The data

Every account becomes 46 weeks of activity

SAML-D is a synthetic bank dataset of 9.5 million transactions. We add up each account's transactions per week into six channels: money out, money in, number of payments out and in, cross-border money, and cash. Normal accounts are picked so they are about as active as the suspicious ones; otherwise spotting launderers would be too easy.

2 · The experiment

Train a detector, hand it over, try to find the people inside

  1. Split the accounts in half15k accounts train the model (members). The other 15k are never shown to it (non-members).
  2. Train five detectorsCatBoost (what banks use), MiniRocket (a time-series model that copies nothing), and three shapelet models: classic, RDST (state of the art) and learned.
  3. Release the modelThe bank shares the fitted model with a vendor, auditor or partner bank. The shapelets go with it.
  4. The attacker searchesFor each shapelet, compare it with every one of the 29,946 accounts and look for an exact match.
  5. Count the damageHow many shapelets match exactly one customer? How many of those customers are flagged launderers? Does the usual privacy test notice?

The usual privacy test scores about 0.5, a coin flip, for every model, so it would call all of them safe. Only a few hundred of 15,000 training accounts leak, and that is too few to move an average.

Adding noise to classic shapelets: how often the closest account is still the right one

Accuracy stays at about 0.88 for every noise level. Exact matches disappear at the smallest noise, but nearest-match linkage needs σ ≥ 0.5 to stop.

5 · What it means

The explanation is the leak

A bank that shares a shapelet model shares pieces of real customers' histories, including who was flagged. The usual privacy test does not see it. Learned shapelets were the most accurate shapelet model and copied nobody, so the fix costs no accuracy.

Caveats: the data is synthetic; the attacker is assumed to hold the same account data as the bank. A weaker-attacker test and a second dataset are the next experiments.
